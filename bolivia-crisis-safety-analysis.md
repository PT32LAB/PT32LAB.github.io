# Bolivia as a Crisis Safe Haven: A Rigorous Analysis

*Research conducted April 2026. All claims are sourced; unverified assertions are explicitly marked.*

---

## Executive Summary

Bolivia has genuine safety advantages during global crises -- geographic remoteness from conflict zones, no nuclear targets, low baseline violence, and indigenous community resilience structures that predate the state. However, the claim that Bolivia is "one of the safest places on Earth" during global crises requires heavy qualification. Bolivia's current economic collapse (reserves at ~$100M, 20%+ inflation, currency crisis), crumbling energy sector, food import dependency (especially wheat), weak healthcare, and fragile infrastructure create vulnerabilities that could amplify -- not buffer -- the effects of a global crisis. The Yungas/Coroico region has specific micro-advantages (year-round food production, water abundance, community cohesion) that make it meaningfully better than urban Bolivia, but it remains isolated and infrastructure-dependent.

**Verdict: Bolivia is a reasonable crisis refuge under specific, narrow conditions -- but it is not in the same tier as New Zealand, Uruguay, Iceland, or Switzerland. It is a "good enough" option for someone already embedded in the country, not an optimal destination for crisis relocation.**

## Part 1: Safety Factors (Strongest to Weakest)

### 1. No Nuclear or Strategic Military Value (STRONG)

Bolivia has zero nuclear weapons, zero foreign military bases, and has signed every major nuclear disarmament treaty (NPT ratified 1970, TPNW ratified 2019). Its constitution explicitly prohibits foreign military bases. It is a member of the Non-Aligned Movement and is not party to NATO or any mutual defense pact that could drag it into a great-power conflict.

In nuclear war targeting models, Bolivia does not appear on any published target list. Studies modeling nuclear winter food production identify South American countries (including Bolivia's neighbors Argentina, Brazil, Paraguay) as among the most likely to sustain civilization post-nuclear exchange.

**Assessment: This is Bolivia's strongest card. In a nuclear conflict scenario, Bolivia is genuinely one of the safer places to be. No strategic targets, no alliance obligations, maximum geographic distance from NATO/Russia/China theaters.**

Sources: [OPANAL Bolivia Profile](https://opanal.org/en/bolivia/), [Bolivia Ratifies Ban Treaty - Arms Control Association](https://www.armscontrol.org/act/2019-09/news-briefs/bolivia-ratifies-ban-treaty), [ICAN Bolivia Profile](https://www.icanw.org/bolivia)

### 2. Geographic Remoteness from Conflict Zones (STRONG)

Bolivia sits in the geographic center of South America, bordered by five countries (Brazil, Argentina, Paraguay, Chile, Peru), none of which are engaged in or likely to enter a major international conflict. It is thousands of kilometers from any active war zone, any great-power projection theater, and any significant naval chokepoint.

South America as a whole has been conflict-free in terms of interstate war since the Cenepa War (Ecuador-Peru, 1995), making the entire continent one of the most peaceful regions on Earth for conventional warfare.

**Assessment: Genuinely strong. The entire continent is a buffer. Bolivia specifically is insulated by multiple layers of neutral or friendly nations.**

Sources: [Foreign Relations of Bolivia - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_relations_of_Bolivia), [Armed Forces of Bolivia - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armed_Forces_of_Bolivia)

**Critical finding: Bolivia's homicide rate is among the lowest in Latin America and comparable to Chile.** During the 2019 political crisis, the overall annual homicide rate actually *declined* from 2018 (4.95 to 4.22), even though the political violence killed approximately 36 people in October-November 2019 (primarily in the Sacaba and Senkata massacres by security forces). **Assessment: Genuinely low violence for the region. Crime does NOT spike catastrophically during crises. However, petty crime against foreigners (pickpocketing, express kidnappings, taxi scams) remains a real concern.**

### 4. Institutional Resilience Through Political Drama (MODERATE)

Bolivia's political track record since 2000:

- **2003**: President Gonzalo Sanchez de Lozada ousted by mass protests (Gas War). Vice President Mesa assumed power constitutionally.
- **2005**: Mesa resigned. Constitutional succession to Senate president, then elections held. Morales won democratically.
- **2019**: Morales ousted after contested election. Interim president Anez took power. Military briefly involved. **Democracy restored within 11 months** -- Arce (MAS) won October 2020 elections in a landslide.
- **2024 (June)**: General Zuniga attempted a coup. Armored vehicles rammed the presidential palace. **Coup collapsed in 3 hours.** Military withdrew, Zuniga arrested same day.
- **2025**: MAS collapsed from 60%+ vote share to 3%. Democratic election produced first-ever runoff. Power transferred peacefully to centrist Paz.

**The case FOR resilience:** Bolivia has had a president ousted (2019), a coup attempt (2024), massive economic crisis (2024-2025), ruling party implosion (2025) -- and through all of it, democratic elections continued, power transferred, and mass civil war never materialized. That is genuinely remarkable for the scale of pressures involved.

**The case AGAINST (fragility):** The 2019 transition involved 36 deaths and military intervention. The 2024 coup, though it failed, happened at all -- tanks in the plaza is not a sign of healthy institutions. The MAS party split caused road blockades that disrupted essential goods supply. Bolivia has had ~40 coups since independence (more than any country on Earth by some counts). The pattern is crisis, near-collapse, and recovery -- not stability.

**Assessment: Bolivia bends but does not break. This is real resilience, but it is the resilience of a system that is constantly tested, not the stability of a system that is rarely threatened. For crisis-safety purposes, the relevant question is: will Bolivia descend into Syria-style collapse? Almost certainly no. Will it be chaotic and uncomfortable? Almost certainly yes.**

Sources: [2019 Bolivian political crisis - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2019_Bolivian_political_crisis), [2024 Bolivian coup attempt - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Bolivian_coup_attempt), [Bolivia 2025 Elections - AS/COA](https://www.as-coa.org/articles/explainer-bolivias-2025-elections)

### 5. Indigenous Community Resilience & Ayllu System (MODERATE)

The ayllu system -- the traditional Andean community structure -- operates on principles of reciprocal labor (ayni/minka), ecological complementarity, and collective resource management that predate the Spanish conquest by centuries. Key features:

- **Reciprocal labor obligations**: Community members must help neighbors with construction or farming, with the understanding that favors are returned. This creates a social safety net independent of money or the state.
- **Subsistence agriculture**: Communities practice diversified subsistence farming (tubers, pseudocereals, animal husbandry) that is partially decoupled from global markets.
- **Crisis buffer**: Indigenous peoples in rural areas are "more sheltered from the immediate negative effects of any economic crisis" because they engage in a combination of subsistence farming and non-agricultural activities.

**However:** The ayllu system primarily operates in highland Andean communities (Quechua and Aymara). It is less relevant in the Yungas (which has Afro-Bolivian and mixed populations) or in lowland eastern Bolivia. Urbanization has weakened traditional structures. And subsistence agriculture provides survival-level resilience, not comfort.

**Assessment: A genuine advantage for rural Bolivia, particularly highland communities. It means that even if the national economy collapses, rural indigenous populations have a fallback subsistence system. For a foreigner in the Yungas, this is relevant only to the extent they are integrated into local community structures.**

Sources: [Ayllu - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayllu), [FAO Bolivia Indigenous Social Protection](https://www.fao.org/social-protection/news-and-events/news/news-detail/fao-initiates-a-project-in-bolivia-to-strengthen-social-protection-for-indigenous-peoples/en), [Social Self-Organization and Resilience in Food Systems - ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/340113211_Social_Self-Organization_and_Social-Ecological_Resilience_in_Food_Systems_Lessons_from_Smallholder_Agriculture_in_Kenya_and_Indigenous_Guarani_Communities_in_Bolivia)

### 6. Sea Logistics Disruption Advantage (MODERATE-WEAK)

The argument: If global sea routes (Suez, Panama, South China Sea) are disrupted, Bolivia -- already landlocked -- suffers less because its economy is already adapted to overland trade.

**Reality check:**
- Bolivia's trade is heavily regional/overland: Brazil (14% of imports), Argentina (11%), Peru (10%) are all accessible by land.
- Bolivia has been actively diversifying from Chilean ports (down from 74.4% to 64.4% of exported tons 2018-2020) toward Peruvian ports and the Paraguay-Parana waterway.
- The argument has some validity: Bolivia would not experience the sudden shock of a maritime nation losing sea access, because it never had it.

**However:** Bolivia's imports as percentage of GDP are ~31%. It imports 60% of its wheat, 86% of its diesel, 54% of its gasoline, and significant quantities of medicine and industrial equipment. These imports arrive via neighboring countries' ports. If *those* countries face sea trade disruptions, the effects cascade to Bolivia with additional landlocked markup (~55.7% higher container costs than Chile).

**Assessment: Partially valid. Bolivia is somewhat pre-adapted to non-maritime trade. But it is NOT self-sufficient -- it depends on neighbors who DO depend on sea trade. The advantage is real but thin.**

Sources: [Bolivia landlocked economic impact - The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/bolivia-landlocked-how-lack-of-ports-hinders-economic-growth-104672), [Bolivia port alternatives - University of Navarra](https://en.unav.edu/web/global-affairs/bolivia-reduce-su-dependencia-de-los-puertos-de-chile-con-alternativas-en-peru-y-la-via-fluvial-atlantica), [Bolivia Trade - OEC](https://oec.world/en/profile/country/bol)

### 7. Resource Endowment (WEAK-MODERATE)

**What Bolivia has:**
- Natural gas (depleting): proven reserves down from 26.7 TCF (2005) to below 8.95 TCF
- Lithium: world's largest known reserves (~21M tons), but virtually zero extraction capacity
- Water: abundant in the Yungas and eastern lowlands, but glacial melt threatens La Paz/El Alto
- Agricultural land: eastern lowlands (Santa Cruz) are productive; Yungas has year-round microclimates
- Solar: some of the highest irradiation levels globally (>6 kWh/m2/day), massive theoretical potential (40 TW)
- Hydroelectric: theoretical potential ~178,000 GWh/yr, only ~2% developed

**What Bolivia lacks:**
- Refining capacity: imports 86% of diesel and 54% of gasoline despite having gas
- Industrial capacity: lithium sits in the ground; no functional extraction at scale
- Capital: foreign reserves collapsed to ~$100M; cannot finance development
- Technical expertise: YPFB (state oil company) failed to maintain production after nationalization

**Assessment: Bolivia has resources *in the ground* but lacks the capacity to *use* them. This is the classic resource curse: theoretical wealth, practical poverty. During a global crisis, unextracted lithium and undeveloped hydroelectric potential provide zero practical benefit. The gas is depleting. The solar panels are not built. This factor is a long-term advantage and a short-term non-factor.**

Sources: [Bolivia Energy - US Trade.gov](https://www.trade.gov/country-commercial-guides/bolivia-energy), [Bolivia Gas Crisis - CEIC Data](https://info.ceicdata.com/bolivias-crisis-a-dollar-shortage-surging-inflation-and-sinking-gas-exports), [Bolivia Electricity Sector - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity_sector_in_Bolivia)

## Part 2: Vulnerability Factors (Most Serious to Least)

### 1. Economic Collapse (CRITICAL)

This is Bolivia's most severe current vulnerability. Key figures:

| Metric | Value | Context |
|--------|-------|---------|
| Foreign reserves | ~$100M (Sept 2025) | Down from $14B a decade ago |
| Net reserves (excl. gold) | $103M | Functionally depleted |
| Parallel dollar rate | 12-15 Bs/$ | Official rate: 6.96 Bs/$ (gap: 70-115%) |
| Inflation | 20-25% (2025) | Up from ~2% in 2022 |

**What this means for crisis safety:** If a global crisis hits while Bolivia is already in economic freefall, the compound effect could be devastating. The currency crisis means imported goods (medicine, fuel, equipment) become unaffordable. The fuel subsidy elimination (December 2025) triggered major protests. Food prices are rising. The economic crisis creates exactly the kind of social pressure that could tip political instability into something more severe.

### 2. Energy Sector Collapse (CRITICAL)

Bolivia's gas production is in terminal decline:

| Year | Production (Mm3/day) | Change |
|------|---------------------|--------|
| 2014 | ~60 | Peak era |
| 2021 | 45.1 | -- |
| 2022 | 41.3 | -8.4% |
| 2023 | 36.3 | -12.1% |
| 2024 | 31.9 (projected) | -12.1% |

Proven reserves: from 26.7 TCF (2005) to below 8.95 TCF. Could be exhausted within a decade.

Argentina ceased importing Bolivian gas in October 2024 (was $600M/year in revenue). Bolivia now imports 86% of its diesel and 54% of its gasoline -- spending $56M/week on fuel imports.

**What this means:** Bolivia transformed from an energy exporter to an energy importer in under a decade. During a global energy crisis, Bolivia would be competing for imported fuel with depleted reserves and a worthless currency. The new road from La Paz to Yungas requires diesel-powered trucks. Electricity generation is 67% natural gas. A fuel crisis is an everything crisis.

### 3. Food Import Dependency (SERIOUS)

Despite having "food sovereignty" in its constitution since 2009, Bolivia is NOT food self-sufficient:

- **Wheat**: imports cover ~60% of domestic consumption (~490,000 tons imported annually out of ~800,000 ton demand)
- **Fresh foods**: >30% of fresh food consumed is imported (including potatoes, which are native to Bolivia)
- **Small-scale agriculture**: ~98% of domestically produced fresh food comes from small-scale family farms, not industrial agriculture
- **Wheat flour**: Bolivia imports 90% of total annual cereal imports as wheat

Bolivia aimed to be food self-sufficient by 2020. It did not achieve this goal.

**Yungas exception:** The Yungas region has year-round growing seasons and produces coffee, coca, citrus, cotton, and various tropical fruits and vegetables. It is more food-resilient than the altiplano or urban areas. But it does not produce wheat, and its population still depends on imported staples transported by road from La Paz.

Sources: [Agriculture in Bolivia - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agriculture_in_Bolivia), [Food sovereignty in Bolivia - Wikipedia](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Food_sovereignty_in_Bolivia), [World Bank Bolivia Agriculture Report](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/739681617168032843/pdf/Tapping-the-Potential-of-Bolivia-s-Agriculture-and-Food-Systems-to-Support-Inclusive-and-Sustainable-Growth.pdf)

### 4. Healthcare System Weakness (SERIOUS)

- WHO ranking: 126th out of 190 countries
- Doctors per 1,000 people: 1.2 (vs. 2.6 global average, 3.5+ in developed countries)
- Hospital beds per 1,000: 1.1
- ~5.9 million people without social health protection
- Quality varies enormously between private (expensive, decent) and public (under-maintained, understaffed)
- No hospitals in Bolivia rank among Latin America's best

**What this means:** During a pandemic, biological event, or crisis-related health emergency, Bolivia's healthcare system would be overwhelmed quickly. During COVID-19, deaths in Bolivia's poorest departments were 7x higher than normal (July 2020 vs. July 2019). In the Yungas specifically, healthcare options are limited to local clinics -- serious medical emergencies require evacuation to La Paz.

Sources: [Best-Country Healthcare Bolivia](https://www.best-country.com/en/south_america/bolivia/medicine), [Health Care Quality in Latin America - Statista](https://www.statista.com/statistics/911442/accessible-healthcare-quality-latin-america-country/), [Poverty and COVID-19 in Bolivia - Lancet](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/langlo/article/PIIS2214-109X(21)00001-2/fulltext)

### 5. Landlocked Dependency (SERIOUS)

Being landlocked is not an advantage -- it is a structural vulnerability:

- Container shipping costs are 55.7% higher than Chile's
- 64.4% of Bolivia's exported tonnage still routes through Chilean ports
- Bolivia has no sovereign port access (ICJ ruled against Bolivia in 2018)
- All imported fuel, medicine, and industrial goods must transit through a neighbor's territory
- If Chile or Peru experience their own crises, Bolivia's supply lines break

The Paraguay-Parana waterway provides an alternative Atlantic route, but it is slow, seasonal, and limited in capacity.

**Assessment: The argument that "Bolivia is already adapted to being landlocked" has some merit, but the underlying reality is that Bolivia pays more for everything, has less reliable supply chains, and is at the mercy of its neighbors' stability. In a regional crisis, this is a force multiplier for suffering.**

Sources: [Bolivia landlocked economic impact - The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/bolivia-landlocked-how-lack-of-ports-hinders-economic-growth-104672), [UNCTAD Bolivia River Port](https://unctad.org/news/bolivian-river-port-offers-landlocked-nation-alternative-route-sea)

### 6. Climate Change & Water Risk (MODERATE-SERIOUS)

- The Tuni-Condoriri glaciers (water source for La Paz/El Alto) lost 39% of their area between 1983-2006
- The Chacaltaya glacier has completely disappeared
- In a normal year, 61% of regional water comes from glacier melt; in drought years, 84%
- The 2016-2017 drought forced water rationing in La Paz/El Alto
- ~1.5 million people depend on glacial meltwater during peak melting season
- Models predict many small glaciers will completely disappear with a 2C rise by 2050

**Yungas exception:** The Yungas region is NOT glacier-dependent. It receives abundant rainfall year-round (up to 1,350mm annually) with groundwater buffers. This is a genuine advantage of the Yungas over the altiplano for long-term water security.

Sources: [Glacier melt La Paz - Aksik](https://www.aksik.org/node/3650), [Bolivia drought - EcoWatch](https://www.ecowatch.com/bolivia-drought-melt-glaciers-2112257923.html), [Tuni glacier - Global Citizen](https://www.globalcitizen.org/en/content/tuni-glacier-melting-in-bolivia/)

### 7. Infrastructure Fragility (MODERATE)

- The original Yungas "Death Road" killed 200-300 people annually before the modern highway was completed in 2006
- The modern La Paz-Coroico highway is vastly improved (two-lane asphalt, guardrails, drainage) but remains vulnerable to landslides during the rainy season (November-March)
- Flash floods exceeding 100mm in 24 hours can trigger landslides that isolate communities
- Road blockades during political protests regularly cut connections between regions (happened during 2019 crisis, 2024 MAS split, 2025 fuel subsidy protests)

**Assessment: The Yungas is reachable by one main road from La Paz. If that road is cut by a landslide or a political blockade, the region is isolated. This is a real vulnerability for anyone who depends on supplies from La Paz or who might need emergency evacuation.**

Sources: [Yungas Road - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yungas_Road), [UK Government Bolivia Travel Advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/safety-and-security)

### 8. Political Instability (MODERATE)

Bolivia's political situation as of early 2026:
- New centrist president Paz took office after the MAS collapsed from 60%+ to 3% of the vote
- Fuel subsidies eliminated in December 2025, triggering protests
- MAS split (Morales vs. Arce) fractured the ruling party; Morales left MAS entirely (February 2025)
- The 2024 coup attempt (failed in 3 hours) was the latest in Bolivia's long history of military interventions
- Indigenous organizations retain massive mobilization capacity and could challenge the new government
- The new administration faces $400M in debt payments due March 2026

**Assessment: Bolivia is in a period of political transition with a new government inheriting an economic disaster. The MAS collapse removes one source of instability (party infighting) but creates another (a large, recently disempowered political base). Historical pattern suggests more disruption is likely, but full-scale collapse is unlikely.**

Sources: [Bolivia 2025 Election - Americas Quarterly](https://www.americasquarterly.org/article/reaction-paz-wins-bolivias-presidency/), [Bolivia Turns to Center - University of Miami](https://news.miami.edu/stories/2025/11/bolivia-turns-to-the-center.html), [ACLED Bolivia Analysis](https://acleddata.com/report/bolivia-braces-tense-elections-ruling-party-implodes)

### 9. Foreigner-Specific Risks (MODERATE)

For a non-Bolivian resident:
- Express kidnappings (forced ATM withdrawals) target foreigners
- Petty crime in urban areas and tourist sites is common
- Suspicion in coca-growing areas (including parts of the Yungas) toward people with cameras/binoculars
- Currency crisis means foreign income is advantageous (earning dollars in a boliviano economy) but also makes you a visible target
- Healthcare emergency evacuation from the Yungas requires road transport to La Paz -- no helicopter services, no local hospitals with advanced care
- Language barrier: Spanish is essential; Aymara/Quechua in rural areas
- Legal system: slow, opaque, corruption-prone

**Assessment: Bolivia is meaningfully safer for Bolivians embedded in community structures than for visible foreigners. A foreigner in Coroico who speaks Spanish, has community relationships, and maintains a low profile is relatively safe. A visibly foreign newcomer during an economic crisis is a target.**

## Part 4: The Yungas/Coroico Specifically

### Advantages Over Other Parts of Bolivia

1. **Year-round food production**: Subtropical climate at 1,500-1,760m altitude with consistent rainfall (up to 1,350mm/year) enables continuous growing seasons. Coffee, citrus, coca, cotton, tropical fruits, root vegetables all grow here.

2. **Water abundance**: Unlike La Paz/El Alto (glacier-dependent), the Yungas receives abundant rainfall throughout the year. Groundwater buffers mitigate dry-season severity. No glacier dependency.

3. **Microclimate diversity**: The extreme altitude variation (1,000-4,650m along the road from La Paz) creates diverse microclimates supporting varied agriculture.

4. **Lower political disruption**: Political crises tend to concentrate in La Paz, El Alto, Cochabamba, and Santa Cruz. The Yungas is peripheral to these conflicts (though road blockades can isolate it).

5. **Community cohesion**: Small-town social structures provide informal support networks.

6. **Coca economy baseline**: Legal coca cultivation provides guaranteed baseline income for Yungas farmers regardless of national economic conditions. The dried coca leaf market was valued at $303-374M (2017), representing 7-8.7% of agricultural GDP.

### Disadvantages / Risks Specific to Yungas/Coroico

1. **Single-road dependency**: One main highway connects the Yungas to La Paz. Landslides during the rainy season (November-March) can cut this road. Political blockades have repeatedly severed this connection.

2. **Landslide risk**: "Extreme events are predominantly tied to the wet season, where convective storms produce localized downpours exceeding 100mm in 24 hours, triggering flash floods, river overflows, and landslides that frequently isolate communities."

3. **No serious healthcare**: Local clinics only. Any major medical emergency requires road evacuation to La Paz (3-4 hour drive in good conditions).

4. **Suspicion toward outsiders**: Coca-growing areas have historically been suspicious of foreigners with cameras or equipment. Community trust must be earned over time.

5. **Not wheat-producing**: The Yungas grows tropical/subtropical crops. Wheat, the staple for bread and flour, must be imported from the altiplano or abroad.

6. **Isolation risk compounds**: During a national crisis, the Yungas could be simultaneously cut off from fuel, medical supplies, imported food staples, and communication -- all at once.

## Part 5: Scenario Analysis

### Scenario A: Global Nuclear War
**Bolivia rating: 8/10** -- Genuinely one of the safer places. No targets, no alliance obligations, geographic isolation. The Yungas specifically would be largely unaffected by direct strikes or fallout. However, nuclear winter effects on global agriculture would eventually impact food imports.

### Scenario B: Global Supply Chain Collapse (maritime disruption)
**Bolivia rating: 4/10** -- Bolivia imports 60% of its wheat, 86% of its diesel, and significant medicine. It depends on neighbors' ports. A global maritime disruption would cascade through Bolivia's supply chains. The Yungas is partially buffered by local food production, but would lose access to fuel, medicine, and wheat.

### Scenario C: Global Financial Crisis
**Bolivia rating: 3/10** -- Bolivia is ALREADY in a financial crisis. A global financial crisis layered on top would be catastrophic. Foreign reserves are near zero. The currency is already in parallel-market freefall. However, subsistence-level rural communities in the Yungas would be less affected than urban populations.

### Scenario D: Regional Political Instability
**Bolivia rating: 5/10** -- Bolivia's institutions bend but do not break. Road blockades and protests cause disruption but not collapse. The Yungas is peripheral to political violence but vulnerable to supply disruption via road blockades.

### Scenario E: Pandemic / Biological Crisis
**Bolivia rating: 3/10** -- Healthcare system is genuinely weak. COVID-19 killed at 7x normal rates in poorest departments. The Yungas has no hospital infrastructure. Rural isolation provides some disease-spread protection but zero treatment capacity.

## Final Assessment

### Is Bolivia a good crisis safe haven?

**For a nuclear war scenario specifically:** Yes, it is genuinely one of the better options globally. No targets, no alliances, geographic remoteness. The Yungas adds water and food security on top.

**For any other type of global crisis:** Bolivia is a mediocre to poor choice compared to alternatives. Its economic fragility, food import dependency, healthcare weakness, and infrastructure limitations create compounding vulnerabilities that could make a global crisis *worse* for Bolivia than for more resilient nations.

**For whom does it work?**
- Someone already embedded in the Yungas community, with local relationships, Spanish fluency, farming knowledge, and minimal dependence on imported goods
- Someone with external income (dollars, remote work) who can benefit from Bolivia's low cost of living while the currency crisis makes imports expensive for locals
- Someone who prioritizes distance from nuclear/military targets above all other considerations

**For whom does it NOT work?**
- Someone dependent on reliable healthcare
- Someone dependent on imported goods, stable currency, or functioning financial systems
- Someone who cannot build local community trust
- Someone who needs reliable infrastructure (roads, electricity, communications)

### Honest bottom line

Bolivia's safety advantages are real but narrow: no nuclear targets, low baseline violence, geographic remoteness, and indigenous community resilience. Its vulnerabilities are broad and currently active: economic collapse, energy depletion, food import dependency, weak healthcare, fragile infrastructure.

The Yungas/Coroico is the best micro-region within Bolivia for crisis resilience due to its water, food production, and community structures -- but it remains dependent on a single road to La Paz and lacks healthcare and industrial supply capacity.

**Bolivia is not "one of the safest places on Earth during global crises." It is a place where you are unlikely to be bombed, but where you might struggle to buy medicine, fuel, or flour.**
